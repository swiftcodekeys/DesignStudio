## 1. Before You Start

### What you'll need

Pulled from the tool lists at Aluminum Fences Direct, DJ Fences, and Ergeon:

| Tool | Why you need it |
|---|---|
| **25–100 ft tape measure** (longer is better for long runs) | Measuring linear footage and gate openings |

### Why accurate measurements matter

DJ Fences puts it plainly: "Accurate numbers stop you from ordering too many posts, panels, or rails. They also prevent running short on materials, which often causes delays and stress." For Grandview customers specifically, here's the dollar math:

- **Ordering the wrong slope tier** means panels that won't sit flat against your grade. If you need Heavy Rack but order Standard, the pickets will stick out the top, the bottom rail will float, and you'll be calling us to make it right.

### Time required

- **Flat, rectangular yard under 150 ft perimeter:** 15–20 minutes

- **Sloped yard, corner lot, or odd shapes:** 45–90 minutes (plan for a second visit if the weather turns)

## 2. Marking Your Fence Line

### Decide where the fence goes

Before you touch a tape measure, you need to know **exactly where your property line is.** Asheville Fence's warning is the clearest we found: "Do you know where your property ends and your neighbor's begins? Don't guess. If your fence goes up on the neighbor's side of the line, it may need to come down and it can be an expensive mistake. Check city or county records or have a land surveyor take a look."

**Check for:**
- **Recorded survey or plat map** — most homeowners got a copy at closing. Your county GIS site will also usually show parcel lines.
- **Physical survey pins** — metal stakes at the corners of your lot, sometimes flush with the ground. A metal detector often finds ones that have been buried by sod.
- **Setback requirements** — most municipalities require fences be set back 6"–5' from the property line. Some cities require front-yard fences be shorter than rear-yard fences.
- **HOA review** — many require you submit fence plans for approval before the first post goes in. Build the timeline for HOA approval into your project.
- **Corner-lot rules** — if you're on a corner, sight-triangle ordinances may cap fence height at 3'–4' in the section nearest the intersection.

### Call 811 before you dig

This is not optional and it is free. Every state runs a One-Call utility-locate service under the national 811 number (https://call811.com). Asheville Fence notes: "The center will notify someone to come to your property and mark the location of public utilities. It's a free service. Asheville Fence calls 811 for every contract we get, prior to installation. It is up to the customer to locate private utilities."

**How it works:**
- Call 811 or submit online at least 2–3 full business days before you plan to dig (some states require more; New York requires 2–10 business days).
- Gas, electric, water, cable, and telecom utilities will come mark their lines with color-coded paint or flags.
- **811 marks public utilities only.** Private lines — like a gas line to your grill, sprinkler systems, invisible dog fence, or low-voltage landscape lighting — are your responsibility to locate. Check your closing packet and look for visible boxes or heads.

**Image/diagram to commission:** The APWA color code chart (red = electric, yellow = gas, orange = comms, blue = water, green = sewer, pink = temp survey, white = proposed excavation) with a cartoon yard showing each.

### Stake your corners and run a string line

Once you know where the fence goes, walk the line with a bag of stakes. Asheville Fence's simple list:

- Mark corners with a rock, stake, or paint.
- Drive a stake at every corner, every gate post, and at the end of each straight run.
- Run string between the stakes, pulled tight. Aluminum Fences Direct specifies: "Use wooden stakes to mark the locations of end posts, corner posts, and gate posts. Run a string line between the stakes to create a straight line."

The string serves two purposes now (a straight measuring baseline) and later during install (a reference for post alignment).

**Common property-line mistakes:**
- Assuming the tree line, the grass line, or an old fence is the property line. It often isn't.
- Relying on the neighbor's word. Get documentation.
- Measuring from the face of the house or from a landmark that might move (a shrub, a potted plant).
- Forgetting to account for setback — you may have the right to the line but still be required to keep the fence 1–2 feet inside it.

## 3. Measuring Total Footage

### Walking the line with a tape

With your stakes in and string pulled:

1. Start at one corner and work around the perimeter in one direction.
2. Pull the tape tight, straight along the string, and read each straight run separately.
3. Write each run down immediately. DJ Fences: "Write each side or section in your notebook so no distance gets lost or guessed."
4. Add all runs to get total linear feet.

DJ Fences example: "two sides of 40 feet and two sides of 20 feet equal 120 feet in total."

For very long runs (over 50 feet), have someone hold the tape at your zero-end or use a measuring wheel — a slack tape measures long.

### Satellite imagery (Google Maps / Earth)

If you can't walk the whole line (snow, terrain, time), the satellite method is a good first-pass estimate. In Google Maps:
1. Right-click your starting corner → "Measure distance."
2. Click each corner in order around your fence line.
3. Google returns total feet.

Google Earth Pro (free) lets you save polygons and gives a more refined measurement including perimeter. This is actually how the Grandview instant-quote tool works — you draw the line on satellite imagery and the tool computes footage.

**Caveat:** Satellite imagery can be 1–3 years out of date, tree canopy often obscures the actual ground, and the image is at a slight angle (not perfectly overhead) at most zoom levels. Use the satellite number as a sanity check against your tape measurement; they should agree within 5%.

### Corners

**Each corner = one post.** Ultra's system uses punched line posts between panels and corner posts at bends. Count your corners so you can verify the post count on your order. A typical suburban rectangular backyard has 4 corner posts (one at each turn) and multiple line posts in between.

**Corners at non-90° angles:** Ultra confirms its panels can shift up to 30° at each post for a rounded or curved appearance (Ultra FAQ #8): "fence panels can be shifted directionally up to 30 degrees; this slight adjustment at each post creates a rounded effect. Tighter curves may be achieved using narrower panels, or standard panels may be cut." For any corner shallower than 60°, call Grandview — you may need adjustable horizontal/vertical rail ends (Ultra's solution per FAQ #8) or custom short panels.

### Slopes do not add footage — measure horizontally

This is the mistake homeowners make most often. **You want the horizontal distance, not the distance measured along the ground up a hill.** A panel is 6 ft wide regardless of slope; if you measure along a 15° slope and get 120 feet, your horizontal footage is actually about 116 feet, and you'll order too many panels.

The fix: on a slope, hold one end of the tape at the ground and hold the other end elevated so the tape is level (check with a line level). Drop a plumb line from the elevated end to the ground and mark — that's the horizontal distance. Or just use the satellite-imagery number, which is inherently horizontal.

The Ergeon "uneven ground" guide is explicit on this: "Panels on a slope may cover slightly less horizontal distance, so adjust your linear feet with care."

### Common measurement mistakes (distilled from DJ Fences, Asheville Fence, Ergeon)

- Measuring along the slope instead of horizontally
- Forgetting to subtract gate openings from the panel count (or forgetting to add them to total footage)
- Doing the math in your head instead of on paper
- Measuring from different reference points on different sides (use the string line consistently)
- Trusting a single measurement — walk each long run twice

## 4. Identifying Slope (Rackability)

This is the single most important — and most skipped — step for a clean fence install. The slope tier you order determines whether the panels can follow the ground or whether you'll have an installer calling you with bad news.

### Why it matters for Ultra

Per FenceTown's Ultra racking reference, Ultra uses three tiers:

| Tier | Max rise per 6-ft panel | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | 0 to 6 inches | Nearly flat ground — rise is within the clearance already built into the standard punch |
| **Rackable** (double-punched posts) | 0 to 20 inches | Moderate slope — the post holes are punched larger so the panel can tilt |
| **Heavy Rack** (triple-punched posts) | 0 to 36 inches | Significant slope — the maximum Ultra offers before you need to stair-step |

**Important nuance:** Ultra's own FAQ #6 says "standard Ultra Aluminum fencings will rack up to 4 inches" — a more conservative number than FenceTown's 6". When your slope is close to the Standard/Rackable line (4"–7" rise per panel), **order Rackable.** It costs a little more per post but eliminates the edge case. Ultra explicitly notes "Rackable and Heavy Rackable options are also available for more severe changes in grade at no extra charge" on the fence panels themselves — the up-charge is on the punched posts, not the panels.

GreatFence's now-archived guidance agrees with FenceTown: "look at your property and identify any short spans (6 or 7 foot spans) that have a rise or fall of more than 6 inches. If you do, then you know you will need to order some of your panels: Rackable and/or Heavy Rackable."

**Image/diagram to commission:** Three side-by-side panel diagrams showing Standard (flat rails), Rackable (rails slanting ~10°), and Heavy Rack (rails slanting ~18°), each labeled with the inch-rise max. This is the single most useful visual aid for this whole guide.

### The Board-and-Level Method (most accurate for DIY)

Works panel-by-panel, measures exactly what you need.

**Tools:** 6-ft straight board (a 2x4 is perfect), 4-ft bubble level, tape measure.

**Steps:**
1. At the uphill end of a 6-ft panel span, rest one end of the board on the ground.
2. Hold the other end of the board so it's pointing toward the downhill end of that panel span.
3. Place the bubble level on top of the board. Raise or lower the downhill end of the board until the bubble is centered.
4. With the board now perfectly horizontal, measure straight down from the bottom of the downhill end of the board to the ground.
5. That measurement — in inches — is your rise per 6-ft panel.

Example: the board's downhill end is 14 inches off the ground. You need **Rackable** panels for that span (14" falls within the 0–20" range).

**Do this for every 6-ft section, not just at the corners.** A yard that's flat at the fence posts can still have a dip in the middle that exceeds your panel's tolerance.

**Common errors:** not using a long enough board (must be 6 ft — the actual panel length); not keeping the board level (check the bubble carefully); measuring from the top of grass instead of firm ground.

### The String-and-Line-Level Method (best for long runs)

This is the method WamBam Fence teaches: "run a level string line from the start to the finish of your fence line. You will need to compensate for this difference in height either equally throughout your fence panels or whatever is most visually pleasing to you."

**Tools:** 100+ ft of mason's line, two stakes, line level, tape measure.

**Steps:**
1. Drive a stake at each end of the run you're measuring. For a whole fence line, use your corner stakes.
2. Tie the string at the bottom of the uphill stake, tight to the ground.
3. Run the string to the downhill stake and tie it at whatever height makes the string level when you clip a line level onto it in the middle.
4. Measure the height of the string above the ground at the downhill stake. That's your total rise.
5. Divide total rise by the number of 6-ft panels in that run to get average rise per panel.

WamBam also provides a slope-to-degrees conversion table (reproduced below):

| Slope (%) | Degrees |
|---|---|
| 1.8% | 1° |
| 3.5% | 2° |
| 5.2% | 3° |
| 7.0% | 4° |
| 8.8% | 5° |
| 10.5% | 6° |
| 14.0% | 8° |
| 17.6% | 10° |
| 28.7% | 16° |
| 36.4% | 20° |

WamBam's example: "A customer has an 11in. increase over a 120in. length fence run. Slope (%) = 11/120 x 100 = 9.2%. According to the chart, the degree is approximately 5.5°."

**For Ultra sizing:** a 9.2% slope means roughly 6.6" per 6-ft panel — that's right at the Standard/Rackable edge. Order Rackable.

**Caveat:** the string-and-line-level method gives you an **average** across the run. If the run has one steep section and one flat section, the average hides the problem. Use this method for a first-pass and the board-and-level method to confirm the steep sections.

### The Phone Clinometer Method (fastest)

Ergeon explicitly recommends this in their slope article: "Use a level or a digital inclinometer to determine the steepness of the slope."

**Apps worth using (free / near-free, accurate when calibrated):**
- **Clinometer** by Android Games Inc. (Google Play): free, shows degrees and percent, uses the phone's accelerometer.
- **Clinometer + bubble level** by Plaincode (Apple App Store): iOS standard, three measurement modes including camera-based.
- **Bubble Level Protractor Clinometer** (jrsoftworx, both platforms): precise protractor plus bubble level.

**How to use for fence slope:**
1. **Calibrate first.** Place the phone flat on a surface you know is level (kitchen counter, a table with a bubble level on it) and tap the calibrate button. An uncalibrated phone can be 2–5° off.
2. Align the phone along a 6-ft straight board laid on the ground across the panel span (same setup as the board-and-level method).
3. Read the degree value.
4. Convert degrees to inch-rise-per-6-ft-panel: rise = 72 × tan(degrees). A 6° slope is 7.6" per panel (Rackable territory).

Rough rule of thumb: 1° ≈ 1.25" per 6-ft panel. So 5° ≈ 6", 16° ≈ 20", 27° ≈ 36".

### The Walk-the-Line Gut-Check (flat yards only)

If you walked the fence line without feeling you were going uphill or downhill, and water doesn't pool anywhere, and the ground looks flat visually — you're almost certainly in Standard territory. But walk it with a level and confirm for at least a couple of panel sections. The gut-check is a sanity test, not a substitute for measurement.

### The Decision Rule

**When in doubt, step up one tier.** The post upgrade from Standard to Rackable is typically $5–$10 per post at wholesale. A 100-ft fence has 16 line posts, so we're talking $80–$160 to de-risk the whole project. If you get to install day and discover your Standard panels won't rack far enough, you're looking at replacement panels (2–3 more weeks lead time) and possibly re-drilling posts in the field. The asymmetry of cost says: **round up.**

### Sloped sections that exceed 36" per panel

If your yard has any panel span where the rise exceeds 36 inches over 6 feet (roughly 27°), no Ultra panel can rack that far. Your options are:

- **Stair-step the panels** — install each panel at a different elevation on the posts, creating a stair pattern. Hoover Fence's guide explains this method: "order end posts instead of line posts, because the second set of routed holes must be higher or lower than the first set in each post. Since it is tricky to estimate the amount of drop or rise, it is best to field route the second set of holes to the desired offset. Each panel could be slightly different for a custom fit."
- **Retaining-wall-and-fence combo** — level out that section of yard before the fence goes in.
- **Flag that section in your order and call Grandview before we send to Ultra.**

## 5. Gates, Obstacles, and Special Situations

### Gate placement and sizing

**Walk gates:** 36"–48" wide is standard residential. A 48" gate fits a wheelbarrow, mower, and most ADA-compliant access needs. Aluminum Fences Direct specifies: "A 4′ wide gate opening (47″ wide gate plus 1/2″ for latch and 1/2″ for hinge) should be a 48″ opening, but we like to add 1/4″ to make the opening 48 1/4″ so the cap doesn't rub against the gate posts. Same 1/4″ added for all single gate openings."

**Drive gates:** Ultra's residential driveway gates typically run 10–12 ft single-swing, or 10–16 ft as double-swing (5–8 ft per leaf). Ultra also offers Estate Gates and Cantilever Gates for wider spans.

**Critical rule: gates don't rack.** Ultra's gates are welded frames (FAQ #9: "Yes, Ultra Aluminum Gates are fastened and 100% welded at all connections and joints for durability and safety"), which means the frame is rigid and can't tilt like a fence panel can. **The ground underneath a gate must be level.** If your gate location is on a slope, you have three options:

1. **Level that spot** (dig, fill, or pave) before install.
2. **Move the gate** to a flatter part of the fence line.
3. **Order a shorter gate with more ground clearance** so it clears the high point.

Plan on 2–3 inches of ground clearance for any swing gate so it doesn't drag in wet weather.

### Trees, roots, utility boxes, rocks

Ergeon's prep checklist: "If there are any obstructions like rocks or tree roots in your fence line, you'll need to remove them. In some cases, you might need to fill in low spots or grade the land to make it more even."

**Practical approach:**
- **Large trees:** Plan the fence to go around, not through. Ultra panels can shift 30° at each post, so you can jog the fence line with two panels to clear a tree and return.
- **Utility boxes (transformers, cable pedestals, gas meters):** Most utilities require a 3–5 ft access clearance. Do not fence one in — you'll be forced to cut the fence if the utility needs to service it. Jog around it.
- **Buried rock / ledge:** Probe your post-hole locations with a piece of rebar before ordering. If you hit ledge, you may need a rotary hammer, a surface-mount post base, or a no-dig drivable post (Aluminum Fences Direct offers these as a common solution).
- **Tree roots:** Smaller than 2" — typically cut safely. Larger — consult an arborist; major roots removed too aggressively can kill the tree and create a liability.

### Pool code

Ultra offers pool-specific fencing designed for BOCA compliance (Ultra FAQ #3): "Ultra designs fencing to meet all the stringent BOCA pool safety codes... Most notably, the bottom rail can be flush with the bottom of the fence, so no pickets are exposed. All pool fence sections come factory pre-assembled in 6' lengths with a broad offering of heights— 48", 54", 60", and 72"."

**Pool code basics (varies by state, verify locally):**
- Minimum 48" fence height from outside grade
- Maximum 4" gap anywhere, including under the bottom rail
- Self-closing, self-latching gate with latch at least 54" above ground (or on the pool side)
- No climbable features on the outside (horizontal rails closer than 45" apart, pickets that can be stepped on)

Ultra's UAF-201 and UAS-101 styles are specifically designed to meet BOCA pool code (FAQ #4). When ordering, explicitly tell Grandview this is a pool fence.

### Corners at odd angles

Per Ultra FAQ #8: panels can shift up to 30° at each post for rounded/curved runs. For sharper angles, "Horizontal and vertical adjustable rail ends are specifically designed to connect to the horizontal rail of the fence panel, making it possible to negotiate sharp angles." Don't try to bend the panel — "panels must not be rounded or bent, as this will compromise the structural integrity."

**If your corner is sharper than 60°**, note it on your sketch and call Grandview. You'll need adjustable rail ends that aren't included in a standard kit.

## 6. Documenting It for the Order

### The diagram

A clean sketch beats a long description. Your sketch should include:

1. **Outline of your lot** with compass direction (north up, if possible).
2. **Your house** and any permanent structures (garage, shed, deck, patio, pool).
3. **The fence line,** drawn as a bold line with every corner and gate marked.
4. **Dimensions of each straight run** in feet (e.g., "Run A-B: 42 ft").
5. **Slope tier per section** labeled directly on that run (e.g., "Run A-B: Standard; Run B-C: Rackable; Run C-D: Heavy Rack").
6. **Gate locations** marked with width and swing direction (e.g., "48" walk gate, swings inward").
7. **Obstacles** marked and labeled (trees, utility boxes, rocks).

Asheville Fence offers a downloadable job-sketch PDF for their own customers (https://ashevillefence.com/images/JobSketch.pdf) — we'd recommend Grandview produce a similar form for download.

**Image/diagram to commission:** An example completed sketch showing a typical backyard with all seven elements labeled. Annotate each element with a callout — "This is a slope tier label. Put it on every run."

### Photos as backup

Take four photos per corner:
1. Looking along the fence line uphill
2. Looking along the fence line downhill
3. Close-up of the ground at the corner
4. The overall corner showing any obstacles

Upload them with your order. If Sarah spots something in the photos that doesn't match your sketch, she'll flag it during the 24-hour review.

### How accurate does it need to be?

Approximate with best-effort honesty. Within ±2 feet on total footage, within one slope tier on rackability. If you're genuinely unsure between Standard and Rackable, order Rackable. If you're unsure between Rackable and Heavy Rack, call (855) FENCE-30 before ordering — that's a more expensive upgrade and we'd rather confirm once than have you over-order.

## 7. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Distilled from DJ Fences, Asheville Fence, Ergeon, and our own notes on Ultra's system:

1. **Not calling 811 before digging.** Hitting a gas line can be fatal and hitting a fiber line can cost you $5,000+. It's free; there is no excuse.
2. **Guessing at the property line.** Always verify with a plat or survey; placing a fence inches over the line has forced teardowns.
3. **Measuring along the slope instead of horizontally.** Produces over-ordered panels.
4. **Skipping corners when counting posts.** Every bend = one post. A rectangular yard needs 4 corner posts plus line posts.
5. **Under-estimating slope.** WamBam warns: "Often the ground slope of your fence line will be much greater than you think it is." Measure — don't eyeball.
6. **Forgetting that gates don't rack.** A gate on a slope will bind or drag. Choose a flat spot.
7. **Not leaving the ¼-inch gate allowance.** A 48" gate in a 48" opening won't fit cleanly.
8. **Doing the math in your head.** Write it down; use a calculator app.
9. **Relying on a single measurement for a long run.** Walk it twice, or use both tape and satellite imagery.
10. **Ignoring HOA or setback rules** until after the fence is ordered. Get approvals first.
11. **Fencing in a utility box.** The utility will make you cut the fence to restore access — at your cost.
12. **Not documenting it.** If it's not in a sketch or photo when the order goes to Ultra, it doesn't exist.
